# HMSC_Moorea_Reef_Fish
This repository holds code, data, and other files related to the third data chapter of Courtney Stuart’s PhD research at the University of Oxford [Seascape Ecology Lab](https://www.oxfordseascapeecologylab.com/) (P.I. Dr Lisa Wedding). 

# Citation
Stuart, C. E., Young, M. A., Davis, A. C. D., Green, S. J., Pittman, S. J., Brooks, A. J., Krumbholz, H., and Wedding, L. M. (In Review). Reef geomorphology outweighs benthic condition and pulse disturbances in shaping coral reef fish communities in Moorea, French Polynesia. Ecography.

# Abstract
Resolving how environmental conditions, phylogenetic history, functional traits, and random spatio-temporal effects influence coral reef fish assemblage structure is important for predicting and managing reef resilience in a changing ocean. Here, we used nearly 20 years of survey data from Moorea, French Polynesia, to quantify the relative contributions of these variables to occurrence patterns for 149 reef fish species. Applying a multivariate hierarchical joint species distribution model, we partitioned community-level variation in occurrence and identified residual species associations. On average, environmental covariates accounted for 88% of the explained variation in species’ occurrence,  while random spatio-temporal effects accounted for the remaining 12%. Reef geomorphic zone (39%) and depth (17%) contributed most strongly, followed by distance to land (14%) and seafloor curvature (12%). Conversely, pulse disturbances, including a tropical cyclone, marine heatwaves, and corallivorous starfish outbreaks each explained <5% of variation at the community level, although responses were detected for a subset of species.  Species’ responses to environmental covariates exhibited an intermediate phylogenetic signal, indicating partial niche conservatism among closely related taxa. Traits associated with foraging, locomotion, habitat use, and reproductive behaviour explained only 1% of variation in species’ occurrence and 7% of variation in species-specific environmental responses, suggesting generally weak trait-based structuring at the community level. Nevertheless, several trait-environment interactions were supported with ≥95% posterior probability, particularly for pulse disturbances and benthic substratum composition. Positive and negative residual co-occurrence was detected among 992 and 366 species pairs, respectively, suggesting potential interspecific interactions such as cooperative foraging and competition. Together, these results indicate that Moorea’s reef fish community is structured primarily by persistent geomorphological conditions and phylogenetically conserved niche relationships, while functional trait variation and acute disturbances play more limited roles. These findings have direct implications for conservation and restoration initiatives seeking to strengthen the long-term resilience of Moorea’s coral reefs.
